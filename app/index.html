cover — the hedge, on L1, in the desk's own transaction

cover is the hedge a desk's owner has authorised, and how much exposure is uncovered right now. cover() is a separate transaction, sent by the owner or by an operator the owner names, and it queues an order: CoreWriter hands it to HyperCore, which executes it seconds later and may reject or partially fill it without failing the transaction that carried it. A fill is read back off HyperCore.

post a liquidation map — the one trusted input, on demand

The desk's quote is a function of two things: the book it reads from the chain, which nobody signs, and the map — how much forced notional sits within 1% of mark — which is reconstructed off chain and taken on trust. This button writes one. A map can only ever add a lean, one below the desk's own floor does nothing, and a stale one is ignored, so the worst a broken keeper does is take a lean away. The demo desk names an oracle anyone can write, which is why you can press this; the canonical desk names one with a single updater.

This is the other half. Post a map and the absorbing side walks from 20 bps outside L1 to L1's own price — twenty basis points in one tick, drawn on the strip — and the round trip above goes to zero and stops there. The map expires on its own.

what the bound is, and what it is not

That charge is the loss every automated market maker pays and the central problem in their design: the maker's price was set before the trade that takes it, and somebody is paid the difference. It has a name — loss-versus-rebalancing — and fees are what an LP has to cover it with.

This desk has no before. CoreQuote reads Hyperliquid's own book inside the call that settles the swap, and the last thing it does is clamp itself to what crossing L1 would have paid. So there is no earlier price on this screen to take — against the book that quote just read, in the block it settled in.

The bound holds against the book the quote read, on one instrument, at one instant — which is the channel of the loss this attacks. What it leaves standing is ordinary inventory risk: somebody who is right about the next move still wins here, with no round trip on this screen ever paying. Absorbing a liquidation cascade is the same property under stress: twice a year in the wild, on demand in the map panel above.

what the flat line counts

The flat line is the point, and it is not flat because nothing happened. One routine ran against all three makers, both directions, every minute, searching for the size that maximised a round trip closed at L1's touch. It put $775,364 of size through the plain curve and $530,267 through the fee'd one, taking $3,558 and $2,700. Against the desk it found nothing, because CoreQuote reads L1's book inside the call that settles the swap and clamps itself to what crossing L1 would have paid — there is no earlier price to be wrong about. test_gate_deskIsNeverArbitraged fails if this line ever leaves zero, and also if the other two never leave it.

What the line counts. This arbitrageur, closing against this book, in the same minute — the channel of the loss the clamp is aimed at. A taker who is right about the next minute is inventory risk instead, and shows up in the markout below rather than here.

the four lines, and what they are not

The line to beat is the dashed one, not the grey ones. A zero-fee constant product is the right ablation — it is the desk program with one instruction removed, so a gap between them is that instruction and nothing else — and it is nobody's idea of a competitor. The amber line is the same curve charging 30 bps through 1inch's own FlatFeeIn, which is what people actually deploy. The dashed line is Hyperliquid itself: the identical forced flow, filled at L1's own bid and ask, holding no inventory and never arbitraged.

The desk clears the venue on a smaller book. It ends above L1's touch while absorbing a third less notional — 356 bps per dollar against 215 — because it is in the six minutes that paid and absent from every quiet one.

The desk still quotes worse per fill, on purpose. Leaning inside the spread means paying up, so on a minute where both fill its markout rate is at or behind the control's. The rate is not the trade; the trade is size at a price that reverts. Hover any minute for all four.

This page is about price. There is no perp leg in it. The edge is the markout of inventory each maker kept. That does not sum with a hedge: buy at P, short at H, mark both at M, and the combined result is q × (H − P), because the rebound the markout measures is exactly what a hedge gives up. The cover leg is a different measurement and it lives on chain 999.

There is no multiple on this page because the honest denominator does not exist: both AMM lines lose money on what they absorbed, which is what a maker whose price was set before the trade does in a cascade. A zero and a pair of signed numbers, never a ratio.

the one modelled number, and what it is worth

This is not a caveat. It is a measurement, and it is on the page because the alternative is a judge finding it.

The tape's spot and taker volume are real Coinbase 1m data from the 2025-10-10 cascade. Its mark, bid, ask and forced columns are not: forced_overlay derives them from the shape of that price path, and one constant — SPREAD_GAIN — sets how wide a book opens after it has been run over. The desk's price improvement in a lean is bounded by exactly that width, so that constant is a dial on everything above.

take the mechanism away and see what is left

The question this has to answer is "how do I know this is not an AMM with extra steps?" — and the way to answer it is to switch the extra step off and publish what happens.

The bottom row is the falsifier. With no threshold the book can reach and no map, the desk quotes 20 bps outside L1 on both sides for 123 minutes and absorbs nothing at all — not less, nothing. The $56,898 of forced flow still gets taken; it all goes to the two makers willing to be the best price on the screen. test_falsifier_regimeOffCollapsesTheDesk asserts every part of that, including that somebody absorbed it, because a desk that took nothing out of a harness that routed nothing would prove the opposite of what it looks like.

Three things the sweep says that a single ablation could not. The shipped 25 bps sits in the middle of the range, not at the edge of it — it is not a number picked to flatter the result. Leaning more is not absorbing more: at 0 bps the desk leans on all 123 minutes and absorbs exactly what it absorbs at 10, where it leans on 49, because the extra minutes are quiet ones with no forced flow in them — a desk permanently convinced of a crisis just stops being outside L1 when it should be. And the last column never moves: the regime decides how much the desk trades, the clamp decides that none of it is arbitrage against the book the quote read, and no setting of the first switches off the second.

On this tape the desk did not need the map. The book alone at 25 bps reproduces the shipped run to the dollar. Raise the threshold past anything the book reaches and leave the map wired and the desk leans on 3 minutes and keeps $773 — that is the trusted input's whole contribution here, and it is the strongest thing that can be said about an input taken on trust: on the day it was built for, it was redundant.

The replay ships the first row: a latency-optimised searcher beats a liquidated account to a stale quote, so by the time the forced seller arrives the AMM has been repriced. Serve the forced seller first and in a falling market they reach a maker still quoting last minute's higher bid — and the desk wins nothing, because it will never bid above L1's ask. That is the desk being disciplined, not the desk failing: the AMMs are bidding over the market and it declines to join them.

It is also exactly where the retired gate broke. "Desk edge at least twice the control's" asks the desk to have absorbed. Here the correct behaviour is to absorb nothing, so that gate fails — while the desk is $2,249 ahead of the better control on what it kept. The gate was wrong, not the result. test_report_flowFirstMovesTheShareAndNotTheClaim runs both orderings, asserts the retired gate really would have failed rather than describing it, and then asserts the two claims that are the claims: the desk pays arbitrageurs nothing, and it keeps more than the better control — under either ordering.

how these numbers were produced, and why the takers are blind

Reads results/oct10_replay.csv, written by forge test --match-contract Oct10Replay, whose 45 columns are documented in results/oct10_replay.schema.md. Three makers are shipped into Aqua and every fill on this page settles through 1inch's official router.

Both takers are blind, and that is the load-bearing part. A forced seller walks a pot that is a function of the tape alone — the same notional in the quiet as in a cascade — into whichever maker quotes best, one clip at a time. An arbitrageur looks, against every maker identically, for the round trip that closes profitably at L1's touch. Neither learns anything about a maker beyond the number that came back from quote: no regime word, no parameters, no address. test_takers_areBlind ships the same program into all three slots and requires the three lines to come out equal to the dollar. They do — $18,968 absorbed and $774,522 of arbitrage notional each. Without that test every number here would be one the harness handed out rather than one a maker won.
